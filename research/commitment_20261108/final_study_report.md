# When does searching for a better prefix-cache order pay off?

**Decision: NO_ADVANTAGE_IN_REGISTERED_SETTING.** I would use the inexpensive cohort-optimality check as a diagnostic and keep the heuristic order for this workload. On 8,925 held-out requests, search found one opportunity to improve on the heuristic selector. It saved 512 prompt positions immediately. Two cohorts later its changed cache cost 2,048 positions, producing a net loss of 1,536. At the tighter cache capacity the two policies consumed exactly the same number of positions across all sampled episodes.

## Question and contribution

Can a scheduler reduce prompt prefill work by searching the legal orders of a small group of waiting requests, after accounting for the cache it leaves for subsequent groups? The proposed contribution is a way to identify **where additional scheduling work can still pay**. An online lower bound can certify that a heuristic order is already optimal from the current cache. A second offline bound applies across all legal orders and retention choices in a complete episode. The gap between these two statements matters: a sequence of locally optimal decisions can leave room for better eviction or a different exit cache.

This question had a reason to invest before observing the data. A long, mostly cached request can displace a fully cached short request. A three-block hand construction demonstrates a real saving from serving the short one first. A separate construction reverses the advantage once the next cohort arrives. The public Mooncake traces contain the request times, prompt lengths and prefix identities needed to test which situation occurs. The competing visualization proposal would ask three case-membership questions to correct a brushed biomedical cohort count. Its robust count bounds are mathematically testable, but the benchmark would require constructed intentions and exceptions and an actual matched-tool LLM comparison for the language claim. The two proposals, their strongest baselines, objections, and the selection made before replay are in the [research contract](research_contract.md).

## Study and estimand

The input consists of two anonymized one-hour samples of production traffic, Kimi conversation and tool/agent requests. We evaluated 16 randomly sampled disjoint time bins per trace, conditional on excluding four development bins out of the original 48. Each bin begins with a common, fixed FCFS/LRU warm-up over its first quarter. Its cache then persists across the measured cohorts. All 32 selected evaluation bins completed, containing 3,014 conversation and 5,911 tool/agent requests. Development used eight of the 40 replayed bins. We froze one configuration and performed no tuning. [Creator release and schema](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/Mooncake/tree/3cca71daccf2a7afb8fe3f0295358f70e3a69fdb/FAST25-release)

A cohort closes at four arrivals or after one second. Requests can move at most three queue positions. The mean intentional collection delay was 102 ms, with a 95th percentile of 1,000 ms. This is an explicit batching cost. The study models the retained prompt cache at request boundaries, with separate transient space for the current input. It conservatively leaves at least the final input token to compute, reuses contiguous full 512-token blocks, and gives every scheduler the same visible requests. The two retained capacities correspond to 65,536 and 16,384 token positions. The longest input including warm-up was 124,847 tokens. Reported capacities refer to retained state only. Generation, GPU parallelism, decode KV and service times would be needed for a live latency comparison.

The primary estimand is prompt positions saved per request by search versus the heuristic selector at 128 blocks. We average requests within each time bin, then bins within each trace, then the two traces equally. The selector simulates FCFS, k-LPM, LPM, queue-aware clustered LPM, and shadow-trie DFS and takes its cheapest immediate order. The search has the same queue-aware eviction and keeps the incumbent on ties. The search/LRU ablation substitutes ordinary recency eviction. Dexter et al.'s k-LPM and PEEK are block-resolution adaptations in this replay. The latter uses a 512-token pioneer key and omits fairness lanes, multi-wave future references and implementation-specific guards. The study identifies the value of extra order search under this declared interface. **Primary paired savings were −0.138 tokens/request**, with an approximate finite-population 95% interval of **[−0.371, +0.096]**. The trace-specific estimates were 0.000 and −0.275. Thirty-one of 32 sampled bins tied. The single changed bin makes the normal/t approximation fragile. Uncertainty here concerns random selection from the 44 nondevelopment bins in each fixed trace. Two traffic samples provide little information about variation among production systems. Sampled variance of the paired effect at 32 blocks was zero, so the registered interval there is unavailable.

## Why search rarely changed an order

From a given cache state, every distinct outstanding prefix block absent from that state requires at least one computation. When the incumbent attains that count, it has minimized work for the visible cohort. This certificate fired at the root of search in **2,452 of 2,453** primary cohorts. Most had real ordering freedom: 2,001 contained four requests. In development, full permutation enumeration agreed with the pruned result for every tested search state. In evaluation, the five-template selector simulated 44,625 transitions; the exact search needed only ten additional transitions at the primary capacity.

We independently reconstructed both saved dispatch sequences for the one differing episode using explicit prefix tuples and leaf-by-leaf eviction. In tool/agent bin 3, zero-based cohort 67 saved one block and cohort 69 lost four. All other cohort costs in that bin matched. This explains the primary result without attributing a latency or quality effect to token counts.

The offline certificate quantifies the remaining uncertainty. Giving free within-cohort sharing, a saved block between consecutive demanding cohorts must occupy one slot across every separating cut. Packing those intervals at capacity C gives a lower bound on unavoidable work for *any* policy respecting the cohort barriers, with corrections for the warm cache and all nonreusable tails. At 128 blocks the equal-weight lower bound was 8,431.1 positions/request versus 8,810.1 for the selector. Thus **379.0 positions/request is a ceiling**, not an attained saving. The simpler distinct-prefix bound was 8,419.6. Cut packing narrowed the bound modestly. The 32-block ceiling was 459.1. This relaxation leaves substantial uncertainty about cross-cohort decisions. In particular, the simpler k-LPM/LRU control already beat the selector at 128 blocks. The selector is a strong immediate-order incumbent, but selecting an order by its immediate cost does not make it the best policy across a trace.

## Scope, cost and reproducibility

The receipts pin 7,445,390 bytes of trace JSONL to their original Git blob IDs and SHA256 hashes. Evaluation included 86,204,698 input positions. The additional 2,984 warm-up requests cost 23,173,016 simulated prefill positions per policy at 128 blocks and 24,588,184 at 32. Development and evaluation replays, including input parsing and verification assertions, took 6.53 and 9.74 process CPU-seconds respectively (6.77 and 9.96 wall-seconds). The summary and independent changed-episode accounting used 0.80 process CPU-seconds after imports. These are local CPU measurements; GPU utilization, end-to-end serving latency, energy and monetary savings were unmeasured. New scheduler submissions, GPU allocation, paid inference calls, repairs, retries, and scientific amendments were all zero. Codex/worker service consumption was unmetered. The cumulative ledger charges the conservative administrative envelopes described in the [closeout checkpoint](checkpoint.json).

The exact pruning argument, the combinatorial bound and a measured reversal are the scoped contributions. Interval packing, longest-prefix ordering and bounded-delay caching have prior art. The anonymization, coarse block granularity, time-bin resets, separate active workspace and four-request cohorts limit transfer. The decision applies to adding within-cohort search to the tested selector. Exit-cache value remains quantitatively open.
